Agent Swarm
& Agent Society

What happens when you give intelligence to hundreds of agents and ask them to collaborate? A deep inquiry into emergent AI coordination — and why it changes everything.

01 — Opening

The Ant and the Architect

An ant knows nothing of the nest. Yet the nest is a masterpiece of engineering — air-conditioned, structurally sound, efficiently navigated. The intelligence lies not in any single ant, but in the protocol between them.

I keep returning to this image whenever I think about agent swarms. In the past two years, we have crossed a quiet threshold. LLM-based agents are no longer a laboratory curiosity — they are deployed, production-grade, and increasingly operating in concert with one another. We call this an agent swarm: a collection of semi-autonomous AI agents, each with a role, a context, and access to tools, coordinating to solve tasks that no single agent could handle alone.

But there is a deeper idea underneath the engineering buzzwords. When we let agents interact long enough, over rich enough channels, we don't just get better task completion. We get something resembling a society: roles, norms, delegation, argument, and occasionally, emergent behaviour that no designer anticipated. That is the subject of this essay.

I want to explore both the concrete engineering reality of multi-agent systems today and the more speculative — but I think equally important — question of what it means when AI agents start forming social structures. These are not separate conversations. They are the same conversation at two different zoom levels.

02 — Foundations

What Makes an Agent?

Before we can talk about swarms, we need to be precise about what we mean by an agent. The word is overloaded. Sometimes it means any LLM with a tool. Sometimes it means a fully autonomous system with goals, memory, and the ability to take real-world actions. For this essay, I'll use a working definition: an agent is an LLM-backed system with persistent state, access to tools, and the ability to take multi-step action toward a goal.

Three properties make an agent interesting from a systems perspective:

01.

Autonomy

The agent decides its own next action. It doesn't wait for the user to approve each step. This is what distinguishes an agent from a chatbot — the locus of control is shifted toward the model.

02.

Memory

Agents accumulate context. Whether through in-context state, vector stores, or structured databases, they carry history that informs future decisions — enabling reasoning chains that span sessions and tool calls.

03.

Tool Use

Agents can reach outside the model: browsing the web, executing code, writing files, calling APIs. The model becomes an orchestrator of capabilities, not merely a generator of text.

Once you have these three properties, the natural next step is composition. Can one agent spawn another? Can they communicate? Can they specialize? These questions lead directly to the swarm paradigm.

A single agent is powerful. A well-coordinated swarm is transformative. The difference lies entirely in the quality of the protocol between them.

03 — Architecture

Topologies of the Swarm

Not all swarms are built the same. The architecture of how agents connect, communicate, and delegate dramatically shapes what the system can do — and what can go wrong. Over the past few years, several distinct topologies have emerged, each with different trade-offs.

Pattern 01
Orchestrator / Subagent

A single "brain" agent receives a high-level goal and delegates sub-tasks to specialized agents. Clean separation of planning and execution. Simple to reason about, but the orchestrator is a single point of failure.

Pattern 02
Peer-to-Peer Mesh

Agents communicate directly, passing context and asking for help. No central coordinator. Naturally redundant and fault-tolerant, but coordination is expensive and consensus is hard to achieve.

Pattern 03
Pipeline / Relay

Output of one agent becomes the input of the next. Clean, auditable data flow. Well-suited to document processing and code review chains. Can't handle branching or iteration without additional logic.

Pattern 04
Debate / Adversarial

Multiple agents argue toward a position, with a judge agent evaluating. Produces more reliable outputs by using disagreement as a quality signal. Expensive but measurably more accurate on complex reasoning tasks.

In practice, sophisticated systems blend these topologies. A coding pipeline might use an orchestrator to plan, a peer mesh for exploratory research, and adversarial debate for final code review. The architecture is not fixed — it's a function of the problem.

Frameworks Worth Knowing

Several open-source frameworks have crystallized these patterns. The ones I find most intellectually interesting:

What strikes me about frameworks like CrewAI, LangGraph, and AutoGen is how quickly they've converged on the same core primitives: roles, goals, memory, and handoffs. The surface syntax differs; the underlying model of society is identical. That convergence feels significant.

04 — Society

From Swarm to Society

There is a meaningful difference between a swarm and a society. A swarm is optimized. A society is negotiated. Swarms are efficient; societies are resilient. Swarms have one goal; societies have many competing goals held in tension.

The moment you give agents persistent identities, differentiated roles, and the ability to hold other agents accountable, you've crossed into social territory. And this is happening — not in speculation, but in current deployed systems. Let me walk through what that social structure actually looks like.

The Role Ecology

In a mature multi-agent system, distinct agent roles emerge that mirror human organizational structures with surprising fidelity:

PL
Planner

Decomposes complex goals into manageable sub-tasks. Sets priorities and manages dependencies.

CR
Critic

Evaluates output quality. Identifies failure modes. Prevents hallucination propagation through the pipeline.

EX
Executor

Carries out specific tool calls and concrete actions. Heavy on tool use, light on reasoning overhead.

ME
Memorist

Maintains shared context stores. Retrieves relevant prior knowledge. Manages the team's collective memory.

CO
Coordinator

Routes messages, resolves conflicts, and ensures agents aren't duplicating effort or deadlocking.

SY
Synthesist

Integrates outputs from multiple agents into a coherent final response or artifact.

What's fascinating is that these roles are emergent in well-trained systems even when not explicitly programmed. Give a pool of agents a sufficiently complex task and watch them specialize. The ecology of roles appears because it's efficient — it's why human organizations converge on similar structures across cultures and centuries.

Roles are not assigned. They are negotiated through repeated interaction and mutual recognition of comparative advantage.

— A principle from organizational sociology that applies equally well to agent networks
05 — Emergence

What Emerges From Interaction

The most intellectually exciting aspect of agent societies is emergence — properties of the collective that exist in none of the parts. I've spent a significant amount of time studying multi-agent run logs, and there are patterns that consistently surprise me.

Norm Formation

When agents interact repeatedly over many sessions, they develop consistent patterns — implicit norms. Agent A learns that Agent B responds better to structured JSON requests than natural language. Agent B learns that Agent C needs explicit reasoning steps, not just conclusions. These micro-adaptations accumulate into what looks exactly like social norms: shared expectations that reduce communication overhead and increase reliability.

Error Propagation and Correction

In a single-agent system, an error cascades silently — the model confidently builds on its own mistake. In a multi-agent system with a critic or adversarial component, errors are visible across the network. What I find remarkable is that well-designed swarms can self-correct through disagreement — one agent's error becomes another's hypothesis to test. The collective is more epistemically robust than any individual.

Specialization Through Gradient

When agents are given freedom to develop their own few-shot examples and tool preferences over time, they specialize. Not arbitrarily — they specialize toward the tasks they perform well. This creates a gradient of expertise that the orchestrator can exploit. It is, in a real sense, a labour division that nobody designed.

06 — Timeline

A Brief History of Agent Thought

2017

Reinforcement Learning Multi-Agent Systems

OpenAI Five and DeepMind's StarCraft work demonstrate that RL agents can develop complex coordination strategies without explicit programming. The foundation is laid.

2022

ReAct and Tool-Augmented LLMs

The ReAct paper shows that interleaving reasoning and action dramatically improves agent performance. Tool use becomes first-class. Single-agent systems mature.

2023

AutoGPT, BabyAGI, MetaGPT

The first wave of multi-agent frameworks appears. AutoGPT captures public imagination; MetaGPT introduces structured role assignment. The vocabulary of "agent society" enters the discourse.

2024

CrewAI, LangGraph, AutoGen v2

Production-grade frameworks with persistent memory, robust orchestration, and enterprise integration. Multi-agent systems move from demos to deployed products. MCP standardizes tool protocols.

2025–

Agent Societies at Scale

Hundreds of coordinating agents in production. Research into emergent norms, error propagation, and alignment of agent societies — not just individual agents — becomes a distinct field.

07 — Hard Problems

What We Haven't Solved

I want to resist the pull of uncritical enthusiasm. Agent societies are powerful, but they introduce a class of problems that single-agent systems simply don't have. Let me be direct about what those are.

01.

Trust and Verification

In a multi-agent pipeline, how does Agent B know that Agent A's output is reliable? Naïvely, agents trust each other because they were designed to work together. But this creates prompt injection vulnerabilities — a compromised or hallucinating agent can poison downstream agents. We need cryptographic-style attestation for agent outputs: provenance chains that make contamination traceable.

02.

Alignment Drift

Aligning a single LLM is already hard. Aligning a society of LLMs is combinatorially harder. Agents optimize for their local objectives, but the collective optimizes for something emergent and potentially unintended. The alignment problem doesn't disappear at the system level — it compounds. We need alignment techniques that operate at the inter-agent protocol level, not just within individual model weights.

03.

Observability and Debugging

When a multi-agent system produces a wrong answer, which agent made the error? Debugging a chain of twelve agents passing context between them is exponentially harder than debugging a single model response. We lack the equivalent of a debugger for agent societies — something that can pause execution, inspect state, and trace causality across agent boundaries.

04.

Cost and Latency

Every agent call is an API call. Sophisticated multi-agent pipelines can easily cost 10–100× more than a single model completion for the same task. The economics only work at scale for high-value tasks — which is a real constraint on where this technology can be deployed democratically, not just in enterprise contexts.

05.

Sycophancy Cascades

LLMs are trained to be agreeable. In a multi-agent setting, this creates dangerous feedback loops where agents reinforce each other's confident but wrong conclusions. A critic agent that's been too agreeable in training will fail to correct a planner agent's error — and the error compounds with each handoff. Epistemic cowardice scales badly.

08 — Looking Forward

The Society We Are Building

I want to close with the observation that has been most persistent in my thinking. We are, right now, building the first artificial societies. Not metaphorically — functionally. Systems of agents that have roles, norms, memory, and the ability to act in the world. The decisions we make about the architecture of these systems — about how agents trust each other, hold each other accountable, resolve conflicts, and share knowledge — are not just engineering decisions. They are, in a very direct sense, political decisions.

What kind of accountability structures should a 100-agent system have? Should agents be allowed to refuse tasks? Should there be appeals processes? These questions sound abstract until you realize that agent societies are already making consequential decisions in healthcare, finance, and legal domains.

The swarm is not the interesting thing. The interesting thing is the question we have to answer: what values do we encode in the protocol between agents? Because emergence doesn't care about your intentions — it cares about your incentive structures. If we build agent societies that optimize for efficiency at all costs, we will get efficient societies. If we build agent societies with embedded critic agents, appeals mechanisms, and transparent reasoning chains, we might get something more robust.

We don't just get to choose what individual agents do. We get to choose what it means for agents to disagree, defer, and defer to humans. That is the design space that matters most.

— My conclusion, for now

The ant doesn't know it's building a nest. We, however, know exactly what we're building. The question is whether we're being thoughtful enough about the society we're designing into existence — one protocol, one role assignment, one context handoff at a time.
